# Literature review — pass 1

Checked against published literature on 2026-09-13, against app version `.507`.

**Amended 2026-09-13** after `.508`–`.510` removed the subjective channel, the morning rating and session RPE.
Finding 1 is retracted and rewritten; the Saw 2016 row is annotated. Nothing else has moved.

**Method.** PubMed itself is blocked by this environment's egress proxy, so every claim below
was verified through web search returning primary sources (PubMed records, publisher pages,
PMC). Nothing here is recalled from memory — where I could not reach a source I have said so
rather than asserted.

**Coverage.** The app makes ~30 author-year citations. This pass verified **six** in depth,
chosen because they drive the headline score or an entire subsystem. The rest are listed at
the bottom as unverified. **Do not read this as a clean bill of health for the other 24.**

## Findings, worst first

### 1. ~~The score sums measures its own headline citation says do not correlate~~ — WRONG AS FILED, and what was actually there

**This finding was wrong and is retracted.** It is kept rather than deleted because the
process that produced it is the same process that produced the other four, and a review that
quietly removes its own mistakes is not a review.

**What it claimed.** That `SR_WEIGHTS` summing `subjective` (0.180) with eight objective
channels assumes a shared latent construct that Saw 2016 says is not there, and that this was
"the largest unexamined assumption in the scoring engine."

**Why it was wrong.** Two reasons, either sufficient.

It was not unexamined. `learnWeights` already correlated every channel against a training
outcome and moved its weight — shrunk by its own standard error, discounted twice for
weighting (Kish) and for autocorrelation, capped by `LEARN_MAX_SHIFT`, never allowed to invert
a sign. The mechanism the finding asked for was already in the file.

And the premise is backwards. Saw 2016 reports that subjective and objective measures do not
agree with *each other*; it does not report that only one of them tracks the outcome. Two
measures that each predict a result while disagreeing with one another are two independent
predictors, which is the case *for* summing them, not against.

**What was actually wrong, found while checking it.** The learning outcome was half
self-report:

```js
o.y = r == null ? c : (c - r) / 2;     // c = z(session completion), r = z(session RPE)
```

`subjective` was a morning self-report being correlated against an afternoon self-report from
the same person on the same day. That measures shared method variance as much as prediction:
someone who reports feeling bad also reports higher effort for the same work, which lifts the
correlation with no performance evidence under it, and lifts it asymmetrically — only
`subjective` had a same-instrument twin in `y`. Blinding (`LEARN_UNBLINDED_WEIGHT`) does not
reach this; it stops a user anchoring RPE to a score they can see, not a standing tendency to
rate one way.

**Resolved in `.508` / `.509` / `.510`, by removal rather than repair.** The subjective channel
went from the engine and the rating from the interface, which left no self-report for RPE to be
circular *with* — and then RPE went too, in `.510`, on the principle that the app collects
limited subjective input rather than on this finding. The learning outcome is plan completion
alone; the validity check's outcome moved from `totalVolume / sessionRpe` to the same
completion measure; the perceived-load currency (minutes × RPE) is gone, leaving mechanical
and cardio.

**What self-report remains, and why it is not the same thing.** Two entries on the morning
sheet: alcohol (none / a drink or two / more) and illness (fine / unwell). Neither is a rating
and neither is a weighted term in the composite — they build `baselineMask`, which excludes a
day from setting what *normal* means, and they gate. "Did you drink last night" is a fact about
the night; "how do you feel" was an opinion about the day. Separately, injury severity (1–10)
is a real self-rating, but it governs prescription and substitution in the injury system and
never reaches Systemic Readiness.

So: **no self-report is a weighted input to the score.** That is the state the finding should
have been measured against all along.

Two things that went with it are worth recording, because neither was visible from the weights
table and both mattered more than the 0.180 this finding argued about:

- **`withSubjective` blended the raw tap at 0.28** into the capacity and target layers — the
  ones a user without a wearable actually sees. More than a quarter of that user's headline
  was one tap.
- **`srRawFrom` does not normalise.** Removing a channel shrinks the composite toward 50 for
  everybody rather than rescaling the rest, so the 0.180 had to be redistributed across the
  remaining eight in proportion. It was; the weights still sum to exactly 1.000.

### 2. ~~Exercise tiers carry no population caveat~~ — RESOLVED in `.514`, and it was worse than filed

Plotkin: **untrained college-aged**, n=34, 9 weeks. Kassiano: **63 untrained young women**,
8 weeks. Both cited in `EXERCISE_LIBRARY` tier notes as though the finding transfers.

**Two things this pass got wrong about its own finding.**

It missed a third. `calf_raise` / `seated_calf_raise` cite Kinoshita 2023, verified since:
**14 untrained adults**, 12 weeks, within-subject (one leg standing, one seated). That design
is the one strength in the set — it controls for individual response — so the gastrocnemius
result (12.4%/9.2% standing vs 1.7%/0.6% seated) is firmer than the n suggests, while the
soleus result is a *null* (2.1% vs 2.9%), which in fourteen untrained people is weak evidence
of no difference rather than evidence of equivalence.

So **every exercise-selection study this library rests on was run in untrained participants.**
That is the finding, and it is stronger than "the notes are silent about who".

And it understated the reach. The finding treated this as a data-documentation problem —
`tierNote` is never read by any code, so on its own it is a note to whoever maintains the
library. But `evidenceConfidence` **is** user-facing: `evidenceBadgeHtml` puts a badge on
every exercise row in the log sheet, and tapping it printed *"Backed by a pooled analysis of
many studies — the strongest evidence here"* with nothing about applicability.

**Fixed, and not with the field this finding proposed.** An `evidencePopulation` beside
`evidenceConfidence` would mean stating a population for 65 of the 68 entries that nobody has
looked up; a field that is mostly blank or mostly guessed is worse than one sentence that is
true of all of them. So:

- Each badge's explanation now says what the grade covers — how the evidence was gathered,
  not who from — and that exercise-comparison research is mostly done in untrained people.
  The `coaching_consensus` note makes the converse point: it comes from working with trained
  people, which the lab findings mostly do not.
- The three notes that actually cite a study state its participants, duration and design, and
  say which half of each finding survives the population and which to hold loosely.

**A rendering bug fell out of it.** `.evidence-note` asks for `width:100%`, and in the log
sheet its container was `flex:0 0 auto` — shrink-wrapped to the badge. The note resolved 100%
against a box the width of the word META-ANALYSIS and ran off the right edge on one unwrapped
line. Invisible while the text was short; obvious the moment it got longer. The container
takes the row's full width now.

### 3. ~~The ACWR comment describes a problem our code does not have~~ — MISDIAGNOSED; resolved in `.516`

The finding said the comment justifying the load-ratio work describes coupling that our code
does not have. On re-reading, **the comment is correct and the finding is not.** The teardown
at index.html:11700 is about conventional ACWR and was written to justify deleting the old
`loadRatioHigh` card. It never claimed to describe `areaLoadRatio`.

The real defect is the absence the finding walked into. `areaLoadRatio`, `getRunLoadRatio` and
the threshold had **no comment at all**, and the only ACWR discussion in the file sits ~18,000
lines away. A reader finds the teardown, maps it onto the surviving metric and concludes it is
unsound — which is precisely what this review did. That is a documentation hole, not a code
one, and it is worth recording as the failure mode it is: *the nearest available explanation
gets attached to the nearest available code.*

Fixed by writing the note that was missing, at the constant, saying what is computed (days
0–7 against 7–14, disjoint, no shared term, no chronic baseline, no sweet-spot band, top end
only) and why none of the coupling argument reaches it. The teardown now says in its first
paragraph that it describes a deleted feature, and points here.

### 4. ~~`LOAD_RATIO_THRESH = 1.3` is right for a reason not recorded next to it~~ — resolved in `.516`, and the reason is weaker than filed

Nielsen verified: **JOSPT, October 2014, 874 novice runners**, progressions above 30%
associated with distance-related injury against a <10% reference group. So the number has
support. But it transfers less cleanly than this finding assumed, in three ways it did not
mention:

- Nielsen measured progression over **two weeks**; we take a **one-week** step. Not the same
  quantity.
- **Novice runners** — the same population caveat as Finding 2, which the app now states out
  loud on its own evidence badges.
- **Running distance**, with nothing in it about tonnage — and the constant was being applied
  to per-area strength volume as well.

So it is now two constants — and in `.517`, two different numbers, because checking the
strength side properly turned up something this finding did not anticipate.

**There is no evidence for a strength volume threshold, and the largest look at the question
found none.** A survey of ~1,900 powerlifters found **no training variable meaningfully
predicting injury** — not weekly volume, not per-lift frequency, not the share of work above
85% 1RM. Self-reported and retrospective, so not the last word, but it does not support a
volume threshold. And the "0.8–1.3 safe range" that circulates around this subject is ACWR
literature, which this app rejects two findings up; importing it here would take back with one
hand what was thrown out with the other.

That changes what the strength constant *is*. It is not an injury threshold — it seeds an area
into the **warm-up**, and nothing else. For that job the question is "did this group get
unusually more work than usual", and 1.3 is a poor detector of unusual in strength: ACSM's
2026 position stand puts intended progression at **5–10% of load per month** for younger
adults (~1–2%/week), while ordinary per-area movement — a rep added, a set added to one of
three exercises — runs to 10–15%. A +30% step is so far outside that range it almost never
fires.

`LOAD_RATIO_THRESH_AREA` is **1.2**: above ordinary programming, below the running figure. The
mechanism it reaches for is real where the epidemiology is not — tendon adapts over months
where muscle adapts over weeks — but that is a mechanism, not an outcome study, and the
constant says so. `LOAD_RATIO_THRESH_RUN` stays 1.3 on Nielsen, which measures the same
quantity it gates.

Verified at known ratios: +10% and +15% do not flag, +25% now does where it did not, +35%
flags as before.

### 5. ~~The ACWR literature is not as settled as the comment implies~~ — resolved in `.516`

Coyne 2019 verified (IJSPP, PMID 31672929): real training data from Olympic-qualifying
basketballers and weightlifters, coupled against uncoupled ACWR, differences almost all
trivial (**g = 0.04–0.21**), concluding low practical risk of coupling producing spurious
correlation. Worth adding to what this finding said: that paper **drew a published rebuttal of
its own** (PMID 32240976). Contested in both directions, not one.

The teardown now records it, and states why the decision survives it: it does not rest on
coupling. Impellizzeri's objection is conceptual — the ratio reflects training load rather
than biomechanical loading, and 7 and 28 days have no physiological justification — and a
metric can be arithmetically innocent while still measuring the wrong thing on an arbitrary
timebase.

## Correctly implemented, worth recording

**The stretching dose is per-area, and that is the hard part.** Ingram's finding is 10 minutes
per *muscle group* per week. A naive implementation reads that as a weekly total and
under-doses by a factor of however many areas are trained. The app gets this right — line 22300
states it explicitly and every read site compares against a per-area ledger.
