# Literature review — pass 1

Checked against published literature on 2026-09-13, against app version `.507`.

**Method.** PubMed itself is blocked by this environment's egress proxy, so every claim below
was verified through web search returning primary sources (PubMed records, publisher pages,
PMC). Nothing here is recalled from memory — where I could not reach a source I have said so
rather than asserted.

**Coverage.** The app makes ~30 author-year citations. This pass verified **six** in depth,
chosen because they drive the headline score or an entire subsystem. The rest are listed at
the bottom as unverified. **Do not read this as a clean bill of health for the other 24.**

Two explicit PMIDs, both real and both accurately summarised. That is worth stating plainly:
in a codebase with this many citations, fabricated ones would be the most damaging possible
defect and there is no sign of any.

## Findings, worst first

### 1. The score sums measures its own headline citation says do not correlate

`SR_WEIGHTS` gives `subjective` 0.180 and eight objective channels 0.820, sums them as
weighted z-scores, and squashes with tanh into one number.

Saw 2016 — the paper the subjective channel is justified by — found that subjective and
objective measures of athlete wellbeing **generally did not correlate**, and that subjective
tracked load with *superior* sensitivity. The app has taken the first half of that finding
(include subjective, weight it highly) and not the second (they are not measuring the same
thing).

Summing non-correlating measures into a single composite assumes a shared latent construct.
If they genuinely diverge, the composite is a weighted average of two different signals and
its value is interpretable as neither.

**Worth considering:** report two numbers rather than one, or use the subjective channel as a
*gate* on the objective composite rather than a term in it. This is a design question, not a
bug — but it is the largest unexamined assumption in the scoring engine.

### 2. Exercise tiers carry no population caveat

Plotkin: **untrained college-aged** participants. Kassiano: **63 untrained young women**.
Both are cited in `EXERCISE_LIBRARY` tier notes as though the finding transfers.

Untrained populations show larger and less exercise-specific hypertrophy than trained ones,
and regional/exercise-selection effects are exactly where transfer is least safe. The tier
notes are accurate about what the studies found and silent about who they found it in.

**Worth considering:** an `evidencePopulation` field beside `evidenceConfidence`. The app
already grades confidence; it does not grade applicability.

### 3. The ACWR comment describes a problem our code does not have

The comment justifying the load-ratio work says the acute window sits inside the chronic one,
producing spurious correlation. True of conventional ACWR — and **not true of what we
compute**. `areaLoadRatio()` compares days 0–7 against days 7–14: disjoint windows, no shared
term, no mathematical coupling.

The implementation is cleaner than its own documentation admits. Left as-is, someone reading
the comment could "fix" a bug that is not there, or conclude the metric is unsound when it is.

### 4. `LOAD_RATIO_THRESH = 1.3` is right for a reason not recorded next to it

1.3 is the ACWR sweet-spot boundary from a literature the app elsewhere rejects, so at the
constant it looks inherited. It is defensible on other grounds: 1.3 is a **+30% week-on-week
increase**, and the app's own running code cites Nielsen et al. 2014 for ">30% is the thing to
actively avoid". Same number, sound reasoning, documented 200 lines away.

### 5. The ACWR literature is not as settled as the comment implies

There is a genuine counter-current — e.g. PMID 31672929 argues mathematical coupling poses low
risk of spurious correlation in practice, since acute and chronic load correlate naturally
regardless. The app's decision still looks right, and Impellizzeri's *conceptual* objection
(ACWR reflects training load, not biomechanical loading; window lengths lack physiological
justification) is the stronger argument anyway. But the comment presents a contested area as
closed.

## Correctly implemented, worth recording

**The stretching dose is per-area, and that is the hard part.** Ingram's finding is 10 minutes
per *muscle group* per week. A naive implementation reads that as a weekly total and
under-doses by a factor of however many areas are trained. The app gets this right — line 22300
states it explicitly and every read site compares against a per-area ledger.
