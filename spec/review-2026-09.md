# Literature review — pass 1

Checked against published literature on 2026-09-13, against app version `.507`.

**Amended 2026-09-13** after `.508`/`.509` removed the subjective channel and the morning rating.
Finding 1 is retracted and rewritten; the Saw 2016 row is annotated. Nothing else has moved.

**Method.** PubMed itself is blocked by this environment's egress proxy, so every claim below
was verified through web search returning primary sources (PubMed records, publisher pages,
PMC). Nothing here is recalled from memory — where I could not reach a source I have said so
rather than asserted.

**Coverage.** The app makes ~30 author-year citations. This pass verified **six** in depth,
chosen because they drive the headline score or an entire subsystem. The rest are listed at
the bottom as unverified. **Do not read this as a clean bill of health for the other 24.**

## Findings, worst first

### 1. ~~The score sums measures its own headline citation says do not correlate~~ — WRONG AS FILED, and what was actually there

**This finding was wrong and is retracted.** It is kept rather than deleted because the
process that produced it is the same process that produced the other four, and a review that
quietly removes its own mistakes is not a review.

**What it claimed.** That `SR_WEIGHTS` summing `subjective` (0.180) with eight objective
channels assumes a shared latent construct that Saw 2016 says is not there, and that this was
"the largest unexamined assumption in the scoring engine."

**Why it was wrong.** Two reasons, either sufficient.

It was not unexamined. `learnWeights` already correlated every channel against a training
outcome and moved its weight — shrunk by its own standard error, discounted twice for
weighting (Kish) and for autocorrelation, capped by `LEARN_MAX_SHIFT`, never allowed to invert
a sign. The mechanism the finding asked for was already in the file.

And the premise is backwards. Saw 2016 reports that subjective and objective measures do not
agree with *each other*; it does not report that only one of them tracks the outcome. Two
measures that each predict a result while disagreeing with one another are two independent
predictors, which is the case *for* summing them, not against.

**What was actually wrong, found while checking it.** The learning outcome was half
self-report:

```js
o.y = r == null ? c : (c - r) / 2;     // c = z(session completion), r = z(session RPE)
```

`subjective` was a morning self-report being correlated against an afternoon self-report from
the same person on the same day. That measures shared method variance as much as prediction:
someone who reports feeling bad also reports higher effort for the same work, which lifts the
correlation with no performance evidence under it, and lifts it asymmetrically — only
`subjective` had a same-instrument twin in `y`. Blinding (`LEARN_UNBLINDED_WEIGHT`) does not
reach this; it stops a user anchoring RPE to a score they can see, not a standing tendency to
rate one way.

**Resolved in `.508` / `.509`, by removal rather than repair.** The subjective channel is gone
from the engine and the rating is gone from the interface, so there is no self-report left to
be circular with. Session RPE stays: it is a post-hoc report of work done rather than a
wellness rating, and it is load-bearing in the blinding analysis and the perceived-load slot.

Two things that went with it are worth recording, because neither was visible from the weights
table and both mattered more than the 0.180 this finding argued about:

- **`withSubjective` blended the raw tap at 0.28** into the capacity and target layers — the
  ones a user without a wearable actually sees. More than a quarter of that user's headline
  was one tap.
- **`srRawFrom` does not normalise.** Removing a channel shrinks the composite toward 50 for
  everybody rather than rescaling the rest, so the 0.180 had to be redistributed across the
  remaining eight in proportion. It was; the weights still sum to exactly 1.000.

### 2. Exercise tiers carry no population caveat

Plotkin: **untrained college-aged** participants. Kassiano: **63 untrained young women**.
Both are cited in `EXERCISE_LIBRARY` tier notes as though the finding transfers.

Untrained populations show larger and less exercise-specific hypertrophy than trained ones,
and regional/exercise-selection effects are exactly where transfer is least safe. The tier
notes are accurate about what the studies found and silent about who they found it in.

**Worth considering:** an `evidencePopulation` field beside `evidenceConfidence`. The app
already grades confidence; it does not grade applicability.

### 3. The ACWR comment describes a problem our code does not have

The comment justifying the load-ratio work says the acute window sits inside the chronic one,
producing spurious correlation. True of conventional ACWR — and **not true of what we
compute**. `areaLoadRatio()` compares days 0–7 against days 7–14: disjoint windows, no shared
term, no mathematical coupling.

The implementation is cleaner than its own documentation admits. Left as-is, someone reading
the comment could "fix" a bug that is not there, or conclude the metric is unsound when it is.

### 4. `LOAD_RATIO_THRESH = 1.3` is right for a reason not recorded next to it

1.3 is the ACWR sweet-spot boundary from a literature the app elsewhere rejects, so at the
constant it looks inherited. It is defensible on other grounds: 1.3 is a **+30% week-on-week
increase**, and the app's own running code cites Nielsen et al. 2014 for ">30% is the thing to
actively avoid". Same number, sound reasoning, documented 200 lines away.

### 5. The ACWR literature is not as settled as the comment implies

There is a genuine counter-current — e.g. PMID 31672929 argues mathematical coupling poses low
risk of spurious correlation in practice, since acute and chronic load correlate naturally
regardless. The app's decision still looks right, and Impellizzeri's *conceptual* objection
(ACWR reflects training load, not biomechanical loading; window lengths lack physiological
justification) is the stronger argument anyway. But the comment presents a contested area as
closed.

## Correctly implemented, worth recording

**The stretching dose is per-area, and that is the hard part.** Ingram's finding is 10 minutes
per *muscle group* per week. A naive implementation reads that as a weekly total and
under-doses by a factor of however many areas are trained. The app gets this right — line 22300
states it explicitly and every read site compares against a per-area ledger.
